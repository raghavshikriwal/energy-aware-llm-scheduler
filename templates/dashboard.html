02 — The Model

How the scheduler thinks

Power ≠ Energy

A GPU's instantaneous wattage tells you almost nothing on its own. What matters is total energy — power draw integrated over the time it actually takes to finish the work. A slow, low-power GPU can burn more energy overall than a fast, power-hungry one, simply by staying on longer.

Marginal, not total

Instead of asking "what's this GPU's total energy bill," the scheduler asks a sharper question for every request: "how much extra energy does adding this one request cost, right now, on this GPU." That distinction is what makes the routing decisions actually correct.

Greedy by design

Every request goes to whichever GPU has the lowest marginal cost at that moment. No global optimization, no lookahead — just a fast, honest, physically grounded decision made fresh for every request.

02.5 — Methodology

The math behind the numbers

This isn't a black box. Every number on this page comes from the formulas below, run against a synthetic request trace. Here's exactly what's computed, what's assumed, and where the model's honest limits are.

Power model

Each GPU's instantaneous power draw scales linearly with its current load, then is scaled by its own efficiency factor:

power_watts = (50 + 2.0 × load / 100) × efficiency_factor

Idle draw is fixed at 50W for every GPU; only the load-dependent term and the efficiency multiplier vary across the fleet.

Throughput & total energy

Completion time depends on a GPU's compute capability relative to a 500 tokens/sec baseline:

completion_hours = (load / (500 × compute_capability)) / 3600

Total energy is power integrated over that time — energy_wh = power_watts × completion_hours — which is why a fast-but-power-hungry GPU can still beat a slow-but-efficient one.

Marginal cost assignment

The energy-aware scheduler never compares whole-workload totals — that grows quadratically and unfairly punishes GPUs already carrying work. Instead, every request is costed as the energy it would add:

marginal_cost = energy(load + request) − energy(load)

Each request goes to whichever GPU has the lowest marginal cost at that instant. No lookahead, no global optimization — just a greedy, physically grounded decision made fresh every time.

The fleet being simulated

Four GPU classes, deliberately spread apart so the scheduler has a real gap to optimize over — from an old, power-hungry, slow card (0.5× efficiency, 0.5× speed) to a latest-generation card that draws more power at peak but finishes so much faster (2.0× efficiency, 3.0× speed) that its total energy can still come out lower. That's the core "power ≠ energy" idea, made concrete.

Assumptions

Power scales linearly with load (no idle floors beyond the fixed base, no thermal throttling or DVFS steps). Requests start processing instantly — no queuing delay or transfer overhead. Each GPU's efficiency and speed are fixed for the run, with no degradation over time.

Current limitations

The load-balance penalty is currently set to zero — this is pure greedy energy minimization, so efficient GPUs can be loaded arbitrarily heavily if that's the cheapest marginal option, which trades off against latency risk. The comparison is against round-robin only (no least-loaded or weighted baseline yet), on a single synthetic trace rather than a real inference log, with no variance reported across multiple runs.

Grounded in current research: energy-aware LLM serving is an active systems research area. See Festina (2026) on profiling-guided energy control for serverless LLM serving, throttLL'eM on SLO-aware GPU frequency scaling, and this workload-based energy modeling study for heterogeneous LLM inference systems — the same "power ≠ energy, minimize the marginal cost" ideas this project explores at a smaller, teachable scale.

04 — The Author

Built by Raghav Shikriwal

BTech IT student at NSUT, building this as a Cloud Computing course project — and turning it into something worth actually shipping. The goal wasn't just to hit a savings percentage, but to get the underlying model right: finding and fixing a subtle bug where the cost function scaled quadratically instead of marginally, then rebuilding the scheduler, API, and interface around the corrected logic.
